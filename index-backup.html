Study Guides

1. What is a composite?

A composite is a material made from two or more distinct constituents. The goal is to create a material system with better performance than the individual parts alone.

Examples: reinforced concrete, plywood, fiberglass, carbon fiber epoxy, bone, and adobe brick.

Key terms: constituent, phase, interface, reinforcement, matrix.

2. Matrix vs. reinforcement

Reinforcement: Usually fibers, particles, whiskers, or flakes. Fibers carry most of the load and add strength/stiffness.

Matrix: The binder phase. It holds fibers in place, transfers load, protects fibers, and helps stop cracks from spreading.

Memory hook: Fiber = skeleton. Matrix = glue/bodyguard/traffic cop.

3. Fiber types

  • Glass fiber: lower cost, corrosion resistant, common in boats, tanks, pipes, and sporting goods.
  • Carbon fiber: high stiffness, high strength, low density, common in aerospace and high-performance parts.
  • Aramid/Kevlar: light, strong, ductile, and excellent for impact or ballistic resistance.
  • Boron/ceramic fibers: specialty high-performance or high-temperature applications.

4. Matrix materials

  • Thermosets: cure by irreversible chemical reaction. Examples: polyester, vinyl ester, epoxy, phenolic, polyimide.
  • Thermoplastics: soften with heat and can be formed/reformed. They are often tougher but harder to impregnate because of higher viscosity.
  • Metal matrices: useful when temperatures are too high for polymers.
  • Ceramic matrices: useful for very high-temperature applications.

5. Manufacturing processes

  • Hand layup: simple, manual, low-volume, good for large parts and prototypes.
  • Spray-up: chopped fiber and resin sprayed onto a mold, lower fiber control.
  • RTM: Resin Transfer Molding; closed mold with resin injection.
  • Pultrusion: long constant cross-section parts.
  • Filament winding: tubes, tanks, pipes, and pressure vessels.
  • Prepreg/autoclave: high-quality aerospace-style processing using controlled resin content, heat, and pressure.

6. Quality, voids, and porosity

Voids are empty spaces inside the composite. Porosity is the percentage or fraction of total volume that is void space.

High void content can reduce strength, stiffness, fatigue life, environmental resistance, and reliability.

7. Damage and repair

Composite damage can include cracks, delamination, impact damage, fiber breakage, matrix cracking, moisture damage, and bond failure.

Repair logic: detect damage, assess severity, choose repair, restore the load path, inspect the repair.
